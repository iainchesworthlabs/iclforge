# Karaoke

A karaoke stream is an ordinary AC-3 programme whose channels are **parts**, not speakers: the
backing music, a guide melody, and one or two vocals. ETSI TS 102 366 Annex C defines it for AC-3;
E-AC-3 carries the same service type and is reproduced the same way ([E-AC-3 and
AC-4](#e-ac-3-and-ac-4)). iclforge handles it end to end, in three places:

| You want to | Use |
|---|---|
| **Play** one, choose which vocals you hear, and sing along to its words | [Hearth](#play-it-in-hearth) |
| **Make** one from a backing track, a guide melody and one or two vocals | [The Forge GUI](#make-one-in-the-forge-gui) |
| Encode, probe and decode one in a script | [`forge`](#from-the-command-line) |

There is no separate karaoke program: Hearth has a Karaoke page, and the Forge GUI has a Karaoke
entry in its Controls switch.

## Try it

[`examples/karaoke/auld-lang-syne/`](https://github.com/iainchesworthlabs/iclforge/tree/main/examples/karaoke/auld-lang-syne)
is a karaoke stream with everything it is made of, so that nothing has to be built first. It is
"Auld Lang Syne", one verse, 16.5 s, generated by a script and not recorded:

- **The parts**: a stereo backing, a melody guide and two vocals as WAV files, and a Forge GUI
  project that opens them in the Karaoke flow. Open the project, press through **Hear** to listen
  to the stream as coded, aware and capable, change a part or a level, and **Save** to encode it
  again.
- **The stream and its words**: `auld-lang-syne.ac3` and `auld-lang-syne.lrc`. Open the stream in
  Hearth and the words follow the singing, syllable by syllable.

The example's own README gives each step, and the `forge encode` line that writes the same stream.

A second example is built for **duets**:
[`examples/karaoke/leave-her-johnny/`](https://github.com/iainchesworthlabs/iclforge/tree/main/examples/karaoke/leave-her-johnny)
is a traditional capstan shanty, one verse, 19.2 s, in which the shantyman and the crew sing
*different* lines and each is silent while the other sings, so that "V1 only", "V2 only" and
"both" each sound different and leave a different part for you to sing. Its project names the four
parts and nothing of how they are laid out; the Karaoke flow and `forge project` work that out.

## What a karaoke stream is

It is a main service with `bsmod` 7 and a coding mode above 1/0. L and R carry the backing; the
slot a programme would use for its centre carries a **guide melody** (M), and the slots it would
use for its surrounds carry **one or two vocals** (V1 and V2):

| Mode | Parts, in coded order |
|---|---|
| 3/0 | L M R |
| 2/1 | L R V1 |
| 3/1 | L M R V1 |
| 2/2 | L R V1 V2 |
| 3/2 | L M R V1 V2 |

A player that knows nothing about karaoke plays the channels where they are coded, so the vocals
come out of the rear speakers. Annex C defines two kinds of decoder that do better:

- A **karaoke-aware** decoder (Table C.2.2) plays L, C and R: the backing, the melody guide in the
  centre, and the vocals mixed in at the surround level the stream carries.
- A **karaoke-capable** decoder (Table C.2.3) does the same and lets the listener choose which
  vocals play: none, V1, V2 or both. With none, what is left is the backing and the guide melody,
  for singing along to. Annex C also leaves it room to move the level of each part, which
  iclforge's capable decoder offers.

### E-AC-3 and AC-4

**E-AC-3** carries the same service type: its `bsmod` is in the `infomdat` group (Annex E, Table
E1.2), and Annex G (Table G.2) gives the E-AC-3 descriptor a karaoke service type. Annex E defines
no reproduction for it. iclforge reproduces an E-AC-3 stream flagged this way as it does an AC-3
one, by analogy and as Dolby's own decoder does ([How it is checked](#how-it-is-checked)), where
the programme is one independent substream with no object layer. A programme with a dependent
substream or an object layer is played as coded: Annex C has no vocals for it. The levels the
reproduction uses are the stream's Lo/Ro mixing group (`lorocmixlev` and `lorosurmixlev`), or
-3 dB each where the stream has none, and `forge eac3-encode` writes the group whenever `bsmod` is
7 and the layout has a centre or a surround. A player that does not apply the reproduction plays
the vocals from the surround speakers, as it does an AC-3 stream.

**AC-4** has no karaoke mode: its specification has no `bsmod` value for it, so there is nothing
to reproduce, and `forge` warns and ignores the choice on an AC-4 stream. [The options for carrying
karaoke in AC-4](https://github.com/iainchesworthlabs/iclforge/blob/main/planning/karaoke-ac4-options.md)
say what its presentations and objects could do; none is built.

## Play it in Hearth

Open a karaoke stream in Hearth (a raw `.ac3` or `.ec3`, or an MP4, Matroska or MPEG-TS file with
an AC-3 or E-AC-3 track) and choose the **Karaoke** page (`Ctrl+7`).

![Hearth's Karaoke page: the stream, its reproduction, the words, and what each speaker plays](screenshots/hearth-karaoke-page.png)

*The "Auld Lang Syne" example, 1.8 s in and held there: the word being sung is part way through its fill. Taken from the running window with `hearth --smoke-shot ... --open ... --seek 1800` ([Hearth's command line](../hearth/index.md#command-line)).*

**Reproduction** is the decoder's choice, kept across starts:

| Choice | What you hear |
|---|---|
| **As coded** | The channels as the stream codes them: the vocals come from the surround speakers. |
| **Aware** (the default) | L, C and R, with the vocals at the stream's own level. On two speakers this is the ordinary Lo/Ro fold, which Annex C says is the same thing. |
| **Capable** | As aware, with **Vocals** set to None, V1, V2 or Both. A single vocal plays in the centre with the melody; Both is V1 on the left and V2 on the right, so a stream with only V1 plays it on the left. A **level** for the melody and for each vocal moves it against the table, from 24 dB down (which is off) to 12 dB up. |

The levels are the capable decoder's alone, 0 dB each is the table as it stands, and they are
kept across starts with the choice. Raising a part far enough lowers the others a little, so that
no speaker is driven past full scale. **Reset levels** puts all three back. A part at the bottom of
its control is off, and the page lists it as muted. A level has nothing to act on for a vocal the
stream lacks or the **Vocals** choice leaves out, and its control is closed.

The page says which part each speaker plays under the choice made, and says in words when the
choice does not apply:

- The stream is **not karaoke**: an ordinary programme, an AC-4 stream, or an E-AC-3 stream flagged
  karaoke whose programme has a dependent substream or an object layer, which Annex C has no
  vocals for. The page says which, and that the stream plays as coded.
- The stream is going to a receiver as a **bitstream**. That happens only with **As coded** chosen
  and a receiver chosen for passthrough in the output picker: the receiver decodes the stream, and
  unless it is karaoke-capable, V1 and V2 play from its surround speakers. With **Aware** or
  **Capable** chosen Hearth decodes the stream itself and sends the receiver PCM, so the choice
  is heard. The page's notice, shown while a bitstream goes out, says both, and has a button to
  choose another output.
- The output is a **network group**: a sink that lists the karaoke settings (and, for an E-AC-3
  stream, `eac3` among the codecs it reproduces karaoke for) and decodes as many channels as the
  stream has is sent the stream as it is and told your choice, and decodes it itself; one that
  does not (an ESP32-C6 decodes two channels) is sent PCM that Hearth has decoded with the
  choice applied. Every member plays one reproduction, at the same levels (see
  [the Hearth page](../hearth/index.md#karaoke)).
- The stereo fold is **Lt/Rt**, for which Annex C defines no reproduction. The page has a button to
  use Lo/Ro, which does.

## Lyrics

Hearth shows the words of the stream playing from a **`.lrc` file with the same name** in the same
folder: `Auld Lang Syne.ac3` is read with `Auld Lang Syne.lrc` (the extension in any case). Where
there is no `.lrc`, a **SubRip `.srt`** of that name is read the same way (see [below](#subrip)),
and failing that an **Advanced SubStation Alpha `.ass`** (see [below](#advanced-substation-alpha)),
and then a **MIDI karaoke `.kar`** (see [below](#midi-karaoke)).

### The file

LRC is an informal format; what Hearth reads is:

```text
[ti:Auld Lang Syne]
[ar:Traditional]
[offset:0]
[00:12.00]<00:12.00>Should <00:12.60>auld <00:13.10>acquaintance <00:14.20>be <00:14.60>forgot,
[00:16.00]And never brought to mind?
```

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `[mm:ss.xx]` | A line, shown from that time. `.xx` is hundredths, `.x` tenths and `.xxx` milliseconds. Several tags give one text at several times (a repeated chorus). |
| `<mm:ss.xx>` before a word | The **word-timed** form: the word starts at that time and runs to the next word's tag, and a line's last word runs to the next line. |
| `[offset:+/-ms]` | Shifts every time. A positive value makes the lyrics appear sooner. |
| `[ti:]` `[ar:]` `[al:]` `[by:]` `[length:mm:ss]` | Kept for display. |
| `V1:` `V2:` `V1+V2:` at the start of a line | A **duet**: which vocal sings the line (see below). Not shown as text. |
| `[v1:name]` `[v2:name]` | What the file calls the two singers. |

The text is **UTF-8**, with or without a byte-order mark. A file in another encoding is not shown,
and the page says so; playback is not affected.

### A duet

A karaoke stream has two vocals, and a duet's lines can say which sings each:

```text
[v1:Shantyman]
[v2:Crew]
[00:12.00]V1: Oh, the times are hard and the wages low
[00:16.00]V2: Leave her, Johnny, leave her
[00:20.00]V1+V2: It's time for us to leave her
```

The marker is the first thing after the time (before any `<mm:ss.xx>` word tag), in either case,
with spaces allowed round the `+` and after the colon. A line with no marker is for nobody in
particular. A plain file with no times may carry the markers too, so that a duet's text can be
marked before it is timed. Hearth's timing window sets each line's singer and the two names as the
file is timed, and saves them in the file (see [Timing the lyrics](../hearth/index.md#timing-the-lyrics)).

Where a file marks its lines, the Karaoke page has two name fields. A name typed there is for this
run of Hearth only, never kept for a song, and comes before the file's `[v1:]` and `[v2:]`, which
come before "V1" and "V2". Every line then says who sings it, three ways: the **name** above it, the
**side** (the second singer's lines are indented, the first's are not) and the **colour**. On a
karaoke stream the name also says whose line it is for you: **guide** when the vocal that is playing
sings it, **you** when it does not, and **shared** when it is a line for both and only one of the
two plays. A sentence under the names says the same for the whole file ("The vocal of Shantyman
plays: you sing the lines of Crew."), which is what choosing **V1**, **V2**, **None** or **Both**
under Capable changes.

![Hearth's Karaoke page for the duet example: each line is tagged with who sings it and whether the playing vocal guides it](screenshots/hearth-karaoke-duet.png)

*"Leave Her, Johnny", 6.3 s in: the crew's line, indented and tagged **guide** because both vocals play.*
### SubRip

A `.srt` file is read as lyrics too, which is what subtitle tools and ripped karaoke subtitles write:

```text
1
00:00:12,000 --> 00:00:15,500
Should auld acquaintance
be forgot

2
00:00:20,250 --> 00:00:22,000
And never brought to mind?
```

Each cue is a line, and it ends where its cue ends, so the gap between two cues is a stretch where
nothing is being sung (which an LRC file, whose lines end where the next begins, cannot say). The
text lines of a cue are joined with a space; styling (`<i>`, `<b>`, `<font ...>`) and `{...}`
override blocks are dropped, and a position after the end time is ignored. The number above a cue
is not needed, a `.` is read for a `,` in a time, and cues out of order are put in order. There are
no words in a SubRip file, so there is no word fill: the whole line is emphasised as it is sung.
The encodings are the LRC reader's: UTF-8 with or without a byte-order mark, or UTF-16 with one.

### Advanced SubStation Alpha

A `.ass` script is read as lyrics too, which is what subtitle-karaoke tools write, and its
karaoke tags are **word times**:

```text
[Events]
Format: Layer, Start, End, Style, Name, MarginL, MarginR, MarginV, Effect, Text
Dialogue: 0,0:00:12.00,0:00:15.50,Default,,0,0,0,,{\k50}Should {\k30}auld {\k80}acquaint{\k20}ance
```

Each `Dialogue:` event is a line from its start to its end. Each `{\k50}` (and `\kf`, `\K`, `\ko`:
the same duration, drawn differently) begins a **syllable** that lasts that many hundredths of a
second after the one before; a syllable is a word of the line, so the fill moves by syllable, as
the script's own sweep does. Everything else is dropped: the other override tags (`{\an8}`,
`{\pos(...)}`), the styles' fonts, colours and positions, and any event that draws (`{\p1}`) or is
a `Comment:`. `\N` is a space. The columns are the script's own `Format:` line, with the v4+ order
where it has none, and an event with no karaoke tags is a line with no words.

Two columns are read for what they say and not for how they look:

- **Who sings.** Where exactly two different **actors** (the `Name` column) are named on the sung
  lines, the ones with karaoke tags, they are the two singers, the one who appears first being the
  first vocal, and their names are the file's. Failing that, where exactly two different
  **styles** are used on the sung lines, those are the two singers; a style's name is its
  author's own label, so it is not shown as a name. One, three or none marks nobody, because it is
  not clear who is who. Hearth's [duet view](#a-duet) then tells each line's singer, as it does for
  a `V1:` or `V2:` marker in an `.lrc`.
- **Which line at one time.** A line written on two layers, an outline under a fill, is one line.
  Where lines in different styles share a start and an end, a line that is not sung and is in a
  style of its own (a translation, a romanisation) is dropped beside the sung one, and where none
  is sung the style that is commonest in the file is the one read. Lines of one style at one time
  are all read, and a line that ends at another time than the sung one is a line of its own.
- **A script made with Aegisub's karaoke templater.** The templater keeps the timed karaoke it was
  given as a `Comment:` event with the Effect `karaoke`, and writes the effects it makes from it as
  `Dialogue:` events with the Effect `fx`, one for each syllable or decoration. Read as they stand,
  the effects would be the lyrics and the real lines would be skipped as comments. So where a
  script has a line whose Effect has `karaoke` in it, that line is read as sung, comment or not,
  and the `fx` lines are not read. The templater's own definitions (a comment whose Effect begins
  `template`, `code` or `mixin`) are never read. A script with no such line reads as it did.

### MIDI karaoke

A `.kar` file (a Standard MIDI File whose syllables are meta events) is read for its words, and only
its words: the music in it is not played or read. What is taken:

- the **words** from the track called `Words`, where there is one, else from the file's Lyric
  events, else from its Text events;
- the **title** and the **artist** from the first and second `@T` lines; the other `@` lines
  (`@K`, `@L`, `@I`, `@V`) are not words;
- a `/` begins a new line, and a backslash a new verse (a line too, here), and so does a line end in the
  text;
- every **syllable** is a word of the line with its own start, from its tick through the file's
  **tempo map** (a tempo change on any track moves every later tick), so the fill moves by syllable. A
  syllable that begins with a space is the start of the next word: the space is joined to the one
  before it. A line ends where the next begins;
- a file whose time is counted in **SMPTE frames** (a frame rate and the ticks in a frame, where the
  other files count ticks in a quarter note) has a tick of a fixed length, and its tempo events mean
  nothing: 24, 25, 29.97 and 30 frames a second are the four rates the format defines, and all
  four are read.

A file with no words, that is cut short, or that counts SMPTE frames at some other rate says so. A
MIDI file carries **no word of its encoding**, and what is read is UTF-8 unless the program is told
the page: Latin-1 and the other code pages are not guessed at, so a Western karaoke file with
accented letters saved in Latin-1 (and Hebrew in Windows-1255) is refused with the reason, as an LRC
file in a code page is, until `forge encode ... lyrics=<file> lyrics-encoding=<page>` says which
page it is in ([`lyrics-encoding=`](../forge/cli/commands.md#lyrics-beside-the-stream-lyrics)). Plain
ASCII is UTF-8, and most are. Only the title, the artist and the words are checked (and, when a page
is named, read in it): the other `@` lines are not shown, so a copyright or key line in a code page
does not refuse a file whose words are fine.

### What is shown

- In a **word-timed** file, a word that has been **sung** is in the accent colour, a word **not yet
  sung** is muted, and the word **being sung** is underlined and fills in the accent from left to
  right as it is sung.
- In a file that times **lines but not words**, the whole line is emphasised as it is sung. No word
  times are invented for it.
- A file with **no times at all** is shown whole and is not followed.
- With **no file**, the page says which name it looks for.

The words follow the position of what is *heard*: the output device's own clock less its path's
delay, so they stay with the sound on a Bluetooth speaker or a receiver with a long path.

They are on the Karaoke page, full screen ([the singer view](#the-singer-view)), and on the Play
page: an item with lyrics, or a CD+G picture, gets a **Lyrics** card there beside the queue (in
a window 1,580 pixels wide or more) or at the top of the monitor column (in a narrower one). An
item with none gets no card, so the Play page is as it was for every other stream.

![Hearth's Play page for a karaoke stream: the queue, and a Lyrics card above what is playing](screenshots/hearth-play-lyrics.png)

*The Play page at its default size, 1.8 s into the example.*

### A CD+G disc

A **`.cdg` file with the same name** is the picture of a karaoke disc that was ripped on its own,
the other half of an "MP3+G" pair: `Song.mp3` and `Song.cdg`. When there is **no lyrics file**,
Hearth shows that picture where the words would be, with the proportions of the disc's 300 by 216
and as large as fits (in whole steps, so the small type stays crisp), drawn against the position of
what is *heard* and moved by the lyric delay as the words are.

The words of a disc are **part of the picture**. Nothing is read from it as text, so there is no
fill, no line to follow and nothing to translate or copy, and the page says so.

**When a lyrics file is beside the stream too**, the words are shown, since they are what a
word-by-word fill and the delay can follow, and a **Show** choice (Words or Picture) appears in the
Lyrics card, and on the bar of the full-screen singer view, to show the disc instead. The choice is
for **this run of Hearth**: it is not kept for the song, it holds from one song to the next while
Hearth is open, and a song with only a lyrics file or only a disc is not changed by it. Choosing
the picture takes nothing from the lyrics: the file is still loaded, so the timing window, the
duet cues and the lyrics a board is sent are as they were. A `.cdg` that cannot be read beside a
lyrics file is not shown and not reported: the words are.

What is drawn is what the disc's own player draws: the screen preset and the border preset, the
tile blocks and their XOR form, the scrolls by a tile (the preset form fills the gap, the copy form
brings back what left), the smooth scrolling offsets within a tile (the picture is shown up to 5
pixels left and 11 up of where its memory has it, which is how a disc scrolls a line a pixel at a
time), the 12-bit colour table of 16 colours, and the transparent colour (drawn as the backdrop, which
is black: there is no video here for it to show). A packet that is not CD+G is skipped
and keeps its place in time, and a file that is not a whole number of 24-byte packets, or has no
CD+G packet in it, is refused with the reason (playback is not affected). The packets are not checked
for parity: a rip has already been through the drive's error correction.

### Keeping the words in sync

**Settings, Playback, Lyric delay** moves the words later (positive) or sooner (negative) by up to a
second in steps of 10 ms, on top of the file's own `[offset:]`. It is kept across starts. A file's
times are rarely right to the frame against a particular output.

### The singer view

`F11`, or **Full screen** on the Karaoke page's lyrics or the Play page's, opens the words alone,
large, on a dark screen. `Esc` leaves it, and `Space` pauses.

![The singer view: the line being sung large, the queue beside it, and the position bar and controls at the bottom](screenshots/hearth-singer-view.png)

A position bar, Previous, Play/Pause, Next, **Queue** and the **Vocals** chooser stay at the
bottom, so a singer can move about in the song, pause, skip, pick the next song or change which
vocals play without leaving. Choosing a vocal there chooses the capable reproduction with it.

**Moving about.** Press the position bar, or drag along it: the words and the clock show the place
the bar is held at, and the song moves there when you let go, whether it was playing or paused.
`Left` and `Right` move it five seconds either way, and stop at the ends.

**The queue.** The strip at the top names the song that comes next. **Queue**, or `Q`, opens the
queue beside the words: the song that is playing is marked, and pressing another plays it from
its start. `Q` or the button closes it again.

## Sing into a microphone

The Karaoke page has a **Microphone** card. It shows you how close your pitch is to the tune while
you sing, and it can keep a take of your voice. It records nothing unless you press **Record a
take**, and nothing leaves your computer.

- **It is off until you turn it on.** Choosing an input does not open it, and neither does having
  used it last time. It closes when you turn it off, when you leave the page, and when the item
  ends or is changed. While it is on the card shows **Microphone on**, and the operating system
  shows its own sign as well.
- **Choose the input** from the list, or take the computer's default. **Refresh** looks again after
  you plug one in.
- **Say whether you are wearing headphones.** Through open speakers the microphone hears the
  backing as well as you, and a pitch score reads as nearly perfect for no singing at all, so the
  score is shown only when you have said you are wearing headphones.
- **The level** shows what the microphone hears. **Too loud** means a sample reached full scale.
  If nothing arrives the card says the microphone may be muted or the wrong input is chosen.

### What it scores against

The card names the part it is scoring you against. A karaoke stream carries its tune in its own
channels, so the **melody guide (M)** is used when the stream has one. If you have taken a vocal away
(Capable, with the vocals you chose), the vocal you took away is the one you are replacing, and that
is the one used. A stream with no clear tune in the part, a stream that is not karaoke, and the
other formats, have **no melody to score against**, and the card says so; it still shows the pitch
you are singing and how sharp or flat it is.

### What the score is

**The share of the tune's time during which your pitch was within a semitone of it, in any octave.**
It hears pitch and nothing else: not the words, not the timing, not the feeling. A singer on pitch
and out of time with the words scores well on it. The card also says when you are consistently
behind or ahead of the tune, which is usually a delay in the microphone and not in you.

Two things on the card change what "within" means, and the card says when either is in use:

- **How close counts** is a semitone unless you choose otherwise: **close** is half a semitone
  (50 cents), and **loose** is a whole tone (200 cents). It is kept, and it applies to the marks and
  the summary as well as the score so far.
- **Score against my key** is for singing a whole song a little sharp or flat of the tune: your own
  key is taken from your first sung notes (it needs about twenty of them) and allowed for, so that you
  are judged on the shape of your melody. The card says how far you are from the tune's key while it
  is in use, and the summary says it was. It is **off every time Hearth starts**, because a score
  that has allowed for your key is not the same score.

### What you see while you sing

- **The pitch ribbon**, under the pitch line on the card and under the words in the singer view
  (`F11`): the tune as a ribbon of notes moving past a mark for *now*, and your voice as a line over
  it. Where your voice is within the tolerance of the tune the line is thick and solid; where it is
  not it is thin and dashed, so the difference does not depend on colour. A voice an octave away is
  drawn on the tune's octave, as it is scored, so the line is where the notes are. When you are not
  voiced the ribbon says **Listening…** and draws no line.
- **A mark under each line you have sung**: three dots and a percentage, in the lyrics on the page
  and in the singer view. Three dots is 75 % or more, two is 45 % or more, one is less. Where the
  levels fall is presentation and not measurement: the percentage is the figure. A line the tune has
  no notes in has **no mark**, which is not a score of zero. A line you were not voiced for (under a
  fifth of its notes' time) says **not sung** in words and carries no number, since that is not the
  same as singing it badly. Without your headphones answer there are no marks at all.
- **A summary** on the card when it ends: **Last performance** gives the pitch score, how much of
  the tune's time you were voiced for, how sharp or flat you were on average and whether you were
  behind or ahead, and what it was compared with: the part, the tolerance, octaves ignored, and that
  lines with no notes were not scored. It stays until you turn the microphone on again or choose
  another song. It is shown there and kept nowhere: nothing is ranked, named or sent.

A song you sang only part of scores low as a whole, because the score is over all of the tune's
time. The marks say which lines were the ones.

### The delay

A microphone's sound reaches the program a little after it was sung, and some take a tenth of a
second or more (a webcam's microphone measured about 130 ms through the whole path, in the Windows
shared mode that Hearth uses). The **Microphone delay** slider is how long, and **Move your timing**
is for the rest: raise the delay if you are told you are behind the tune and are not.

### A take

With the microphone on, **Record a take** keeps your voice. The card shows **Recording** and how long
it has run, which is a second sign beside **Microphone on**, since having the microphone on is not
recording. **Stop recording** keeps it, and turning the microphone off, leaving the page or
the item ending does the same.

- **It is your voice alone**, not the backing: the microphone's own sound mixed to one channel, at the
  microphone's rate, as a lossless FLAC file at 24 bits. It can be placed against any copy of the
  backing and re-mixed. Through open speakers the backing is in it too, which is the other reason
  to wear headphones.
- **A small file beside it**, `Song - 2026-10-12 211500.take.json`, says which item it was, where
  in the item its first sample was sung (taken from where the song was when the sound reached the
  microphone, less the microphone's delay, plus the timing you moved), and the alignment in force,
  so that any editor can place it and you can move it if you disagree.
- **Where it goes** is shown on the card and changed with **Choose…**: your documents, in a folder
  called "Hearth takes", unless you chose another. The folder is not made until the first take.
  Takes are never put beside the song file.
- **The last take** is offered with **Show in folder** and **Delete this take**, which removes the
  file and its description.
- **Your takes** lists what is in the folder, newest first: the five newest, and how many more
  there are. Each has **Delete**, which asks again (**Delete for good**, or **Keep**) before it
  removes the file and its description, because a take cannot be got back. Only takes Hearth made
  are listed: a file with no description beside it is not one, and is never offered for removal.

### Make a karaoke stream with your voice in it

A take is a vocal part, and the Forge's [Karaoke flow](../forge/gui/karaoke.md) takes any mono file
as **Vocal 1** or **Vocal 2**. A take is a recording of one person at one time, so four things need
handling first:

- **It is FLAC, and the Forge reads WAV.** Convert it with any tool that reads FLAC; the lines below
  use ffmpeg.
- **It is at the microphone's rate**, often 44.1 kHz. The Forge GUI resamples a part to the
  backing's rate and says so; `forge encode` refuses a source at another rate, so convert it.
- **It starts partway into the song.** `item_start_ms` in the `.take.json` is where, in
  milliseconds (12340 is 12.34 s), with the microphone's delay and any timing you moved already
  taken off. `null` means the take could not be placed, and you place it by ear.
- **It is shorter than the song.** The Forge holds a part's last sample past the part's end, which
  is not silence: end the take on silence and as long as the backing. The command below does.

In the Forge GUI, give the backing and the melody guide as their parts, the take converted to WAV
as **Vocal 1**, set its **Start offset** to `item_start_ms` divided by 1000, and save.

From the command line, from a stream you sang to (a 3/2 stream with a melody guide and two vocals:
`forge probe` says the layout) and a take:

```text
forge decode song.ac3 parts.wav
ffmpeg -i "Song - 2026-10-12 211500.flac" -af "adelay=12340,apad=whole_dur=213.5" -ar 48000 -c:a pcm_s24le take.wav
forge encode parts.wav mine.ac3 320 L,C,R,Cs src=take.wav map=0.0:L,0.1:R,0.2:C,0.3:none,0.4:none,1.0:Cs bsmod=voiceover
```

`adelay` is in milliseconds (the take's `item_start_ms`) and `apad=whole_dur` is the song's length
in seconds (`forge probe` shows it as the duration); together they make the take as long as the
backing, silent before and after it. `-ar` is the backing's rate. In `forge encode`, `parts.wav`
is source 0: its channels 0, 1 and 2 are L, R and the melody guide, and channels 3 and 4, the
stream's own vocals, go to `none`. The take is source 1 and takes the surround, which one vocal
makes a 3/1 stream with (`L,C,R,Cs`, 320 kbit/s). A stream with one vocal has no channel 4, so leave
`0.4:none` out.

This decodes the stream and encodes it again, which is a second generation of AC-3 for the backing.
If you still have the files it was made from, give those to the Karaoke flow instead.
A one-click "save my performance as a karaoke stream" in Hearth is not built: the plan holds it
until this has been tried ([D12](https://github.com/iainchesworthlabs/iclforge/blob/main/planning/karaoke-microphone.md)).
A take made through open speakers has the backing in it as well as your voice, and is a recording of
somebody else's work: keep it for yourself.

### What it does not do

- It does not play your voice back to you. A microphone through the operating system's own audio
  path adds a tenth of a second on common equipment, which is too late to sing along with; use your
  headset's own monitoring if it has one.
- It does not send your voice to other speakers or other devices.
- It cannot tell a singer from a loud speaker, which is why the headphones answer matters.

## Make one in the Forge GUI

Choose **Karaoke** in the Controls switch. Four steps build the stream from the recordings it is
made of:

![The Forge GUI's Karaoke flow at the Parts step: a backing, a melody guide and two vocals, each its own file](screenshots/forge-karaoke-parts.png)

1. **Parts.** Give each part its own file: a **stereo backing**, and optionally a **mono melody
   guide**, **Vocal 1** and **Vocal 2**. Or give **one multichannel file** whose WAV speakers are
   already named. The page shows the coding mode the parts make, why, and what is wrong with
   anything that cannot be used.
2. **Levels.** The centre level (the melody guide in a stereo fold) and the surround level (the
   vocals), which an aware decoder applies.
3. **Hear.** The stream is encoded and played back the way a karaoke player decodes it: as coded,
   aware, or capable with the vocals you choose. It plays the encoded stream, not your source
   files.
4. **Save.** The format (AC-3, the default, or E-AC-3), the file type (the raw stream, or
   Matroska, MP4 or MPEG-TS), the bit rate, the equivalent `forge encode` (or `forge eac3-encode`)
   command, and the encode.

![The Hear step: the stream built, played as an aware decoder plays it, with which part reaches which speaker](screenshots/forge-karaoke-hear.png)

Each coded channel is written into the same draft the other tiers edit, so switching to Expert
afterwards shows the same bed, `bsmod` and assignments. The Forge GUI guide has the details of each
step and of what a part may be: [Forge GUI, Karaoke](../forge/gui/karaoke.md).

## From the command line

Encode: a stereo backing and a melody guide and two vocals as five coded channels, with `bsmod`
set to 7 (the token is `voiceover`, which at a layout above 1/0 is the karaoke main service). The
sources are mapped to bed positions with `src=` and `map=`:

```text
forge encode backing.wav out.ac3 384 L,C,R,Ls,Rs src=melody.wav src=lead.wav src=harmony.wav \
      map=0.0:L,0.1:R,1.0:C,2.0:Ls,3.0:Rs bsmod=voiceover
```

A single multichannel WAV whose speakers are named needs no `map=`.

E-AC-3 takes the same sources, mapping and `bsmod`. `forge eac3-encode` has its coding tools
positional (`none` here) before the layout, and writes the stream's Lo/Ro mixing group, which is
where a decoder reads the levels of the reproduction:

```text
forge eac3-encode backing.wav out.ec3 384 none L,C,R,Ls,Rs src=melody.wav src=lead.wav src=harmony.wav \
      map=0.0:L,0.1:R,1.0:C,2.0:Ls,3.0:Rs bsmod=voiceover
```

Probe:

```text
$ forge probe out.ac3
...
bsmod           7 (karaoke)
layout          3/2 (acmod 7, lfeon 0)
renders         5 channel(s): L C R Ls Rs
```

Decode, as an aware decoder (`karaoke`) or a capable one with a choice of vocals
(`karaoke=none`, `karaoke=v1`, `karaoke=v2` or `karaoke=v1+v2`), to L, C and R:

```text
forge decode out.ac3 aware.wav karaoke
forge decode out.ac3 melody-and-v1.wav karaoke=v1
```

`channels=2` or `channels=1` gives the 2/0 or 1/0 reproduction. `downmix=ltrt` is refused with
`karaoke`, as Annex C defines no Lt/Rt reproduction. A stream that is not karaoke is decoded as
coded, and the report says so. An E-AC-3 karaoke stream decodes the same way
(`forge decode out.ec3 aware.wav karaoke`), and the report says the reproduction is applied to
E-AC-3 as Dolby's decoder does. The library form is `OutputConfig::karaoke` and
`OutputConfig::karaoke_vocals`; see [Decoding](../library/decoding.md).

`forge monitor` takes the same choice and plays the reproduction on the output:

```text
forge monitor out.ac3 karaoke=none      # L, C, R: the backing and the melody, for singing along
forge monitor out.ac3 karaoke=v1+v2     # V1 left, V2 right
```

The capable decoder's levels are `karaoke-melody=<dB>`, `karaoke-v1=<dB>` and `karaoke-v2=<dB>` on
both commands: each part against Table C.2.3, 0 dB the table, up to 12 dB louder and `-24` or `off`
at the bottom, the span and the defaults of Hearth's sliders.

```text
forge decode out.ac3 sing.wav karaoke=none karaoke-melody=-6
forge monitor out.ac3 karaoke=v1 karaoke-v1=-12 karaoke-melody=3
```

They move nothing under the aware reproduction, so a level without a choice of vocals
(`karaoke=none`, `v1`, `v2` or `v1+v2`) is refused.

On an output narrower than the stream, which `monitor` folds to unasked, the 2/0 reproduction
plays. `forge play` applies the choice where it decodes (its PCM fallback), and `forge spdif`
and `forge play`'s bitstream legs hand the coded stream on: they accept `karaoke` and say it is
not applied, since a receiver reproduces the stream. AC-4 is ignored with a warning by all of
them, and `forge spatial` ignores the choice with its own: it places an E-AC-3 stream's objects
and has no karaoke bed.

## From a program

The reproduction and the lyric reader are in the libraries, so a program of your own can use them:

| You are in | The reproduction | The lyrics |
|---|---|---|
| C or C++ through the C API | `iclforge_decoder_create_with_karaoke()`, [C API](../library/c-api.md#karaoke-streams-annex-c) | `iclforge_lyrics_parse()` and its cursor, [C API](../library/c-api.md#lyrics) |
| Python | `DecoderConfig(karaoke=...)`, [Python](../library/python-api.md#karaoke-streams-and-lyrics) | `iclforge.lyrics` |
| Rust | `Decoder::with_karaoke`, [Rust](../library/rust-api.md#karaoke-and-lyrics) | `iclforge::lyrics::Lyrics` |
| A browser or Node (WebAssembly) | `FoldOptions.karaoke`, [the npm package](../platforms/wasm.md) | `parseLyrics` in the demo's module, which the [decode demo](../wasm-demo.md)'s Words panel uses |
| C++ | `OutputConfig::karaoke` and its levels, [Decoding](../library/decoding.md) | `iclforge::lyrics`, `libs/lyrics` |

All of them take the same choices (as coded, aware or capable; the vocals; the three part levels in
dB over -24 to +12, where the bottom is off) and the same bytes of a lyrics file, and give the same
samples and the same lines as `forge decode` and Hearth do.

## What it does not do

- **Aligning words to the sound.** Hearth's timing window ([Timing the
  lyrics](../hearth/index.md#timing-the-lyrics)) takes word times from your presses, and an estimate
  by the letters of a word is only where a press starts from. A forced aligner is outside software,
  and [Timing a song with a forced aligner](forced-alignment.md) runs one and reads its result in.
- **Reading a lyrics file in a code page, in Hearth and Crucible.** UTF-8 and UTF-16 are read there;
  Latin-1, Windows-1255 and the other encodings are named and not guessed at. Forge reads one when it
  is told the page (`lyrics-encoding=`, or the Code page list on the Karaoke flow's Lyrics row, which
  shows how the page chosen reads the first lines) and writes the words beside the stream as UTF-8,
  which every player reads, so a file in a code page is brought in once, at the encode. A MIDI
  karaoke file has no way to say its encoding; naming the page is how to read it.
- **Text from a CD+G disc.** Its words are pixels, so Hearth shows the picture and cannot follow the
  words.
- **A screen on a sink that has not been run.** A board that decodes for itself is sent the lyrics
  and where the sound is in them, follows them on its own clock and reports the line. The P4
  firmware also draws them, with the colour moving as the song does, on a 7-inch panel
  ([An ESP32-P4 sink](../hearth/sink-esp32-p4.md#the-screen)): drawn and measured on a board with no
  panel, not yet run on one, and without a microphone, scoring or recording.
- **Karaoke in AC-4.** Its specification has no karaoke mode, so nothing is reproduced for it.
- **Knowing what a receiver does with E-AC-3.** Annex E defines no reproduction and no TV or
  receiver was tried here: Dolby's reference decoder reproduces an E-AC-3 karaoke stream, and a
  player that does not reproduce it plays the vocals from the surrounds.
- **A microphone on a board or on Android.** The microphone, the pitch score and the takes are in
  Hearth's desktop player and the browser demo ([Sing into a microphone](#sing-into-a-microphone)).
  The board's part of the [plan](https://github.com/iainchesworthlabs/iclforge/blob/main/planning/karaoke-microphone.md)
  is not built.

## How it is checked

Two things stand behind it. The reproduction is held to the annex's own tables, transcribed
independently of the code: every mode is encoded with a tone in each part and decoded under each
reproduction and each choice of vocals, and the tones on each output are what the table says. And
Dolby's reference decoder reproduces karaoke for AC-3 and for E-AC-3, which is the one outside
decoder to compare with: `tools/checks/verify_karaoke_oracle.py` encodes a tone-per-channel stream
both ways, decodes it with `forge decode` and with the Reference Player's `dlbac3dec`, and compares
what each output channel carries as ratios. The player is licensed, so the script runs locally and
not in CI, and its measured ratios are constants in the library's tests
(`libs/ac3/tests/decoder/test_eac3_karaoke.cpp`). There is still no karaoke stream from anyone
else: Dolby's encoder (DEE) cannot write `bsmod` 7. See [Verification](../verification.md).
